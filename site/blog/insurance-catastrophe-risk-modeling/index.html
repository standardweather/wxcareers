Careers in insurance and catastrophe risk modeling

What meteorologists do in catastrophe risk modeling at insurers, reinsurers, brokers, and modeling firms, and how to get into the work.

Property insurers live with weather risk. The practical question is how often a hurricane, hailstorm, flood, or wildfire will produce large losses, and how large those losses can get. Meteorologists work on that problem at catastrophe modeling firms, reinsurers and brokers, and insurers' risk teams.

NOAA's National Centers for Environmental Information counted 27 U.S. weather and climate disasters in 2024 that each caused at least $1 billion in damages, with a total cost of about $182.7 billion, including uninsured damage. On the insurance side, the Insurance Information Institute, using Aon data, reports $103.1 billion in U.S. insured catastrophe losses in 2025. Severe convective storms accounted for $52.3 billion of that total, more than any other peril.

Where the jobs sit

The American Meteorological Society's insurance career guide puts it plainly: almost every property insurance company is a weather company. That need runs through primary insurers, reinsurers, and the firms between them. Companies also ask whether the probabilities they use still hold as the climate changes.

Catastrophe modeling firms build and maintain the models that estimate those losses. Moody's describes models covering hurricanes and other tropical cyclones, severe convective storms, flood, and wildfire, among other perils, and says insurers and reinsurers use them to price policies, set reserves, and buy reinsurance. Verisk describes the same purpose: estimate potential losses by combining hazard information, data on insured properties, and how those properties respond to damage.

Reinsurers and brokers often hire scientists to evaluate those vendor models and form an independent view of risk. Aon describes a model evaluation team of more than 20 engineers and scientists with expertise in meteorology, hydrology, and climate science who assess vendor models by peril and region. Insurers' own catastrophe and risk teams run models against their books of business and support underwriting. A Berkshire Hathaway Specialty Insurance posting for a catastrophe risk analyst describes calculating risk metrics for atmospheric perils, briefing underwriters, and analyzing meteorological observations, claims data, and hazard models.

What the work involves

The core product is not a forecast for a particular day. It is a view of risk over many possible events. Moody's outlines a common four-part framework: an event module that builds a large set of simulated scenarios, a hazard module that estimates conditions such as wind at locations at risk, a vulnerability module that estimates damage to buildings and contents, and a financial module that turns damage into monetary loss. Verisk describes a similar sequence, from event generation through hazard, exposure, damage, and insured loss. The metrics that come out of those runs include average annual loss and exceedance probability, the chance that a loss will exceed a given amount in a year. A 1 percent exceedance probability is more accurately described as a 1-in-100 chance of being exceeded this year, not as a promise that the loss happens once every 100 years.

The perils line up with the loss record. From 1980 through 2024, tropical cyclones caused the most damage among U.S. billion-dollar disasters, $1,543.2 billion, and the highest average event cost, $23.0 billion per event. Severe storms were the most frequent type, with 203 events, but a much lower average cost of $2.5 billion. Inland flooding added $203.0 billion over the same period. The insurance work covers those hazards and wildfire, including how wind, surge, hail, tornadoes, and fire spread translate into damage at a location.

Event response is the other rhythm of the job. When a storm is in progress, teams monitor it, run models with the latest track or footprint, and produce loss estimates and briefings. A Moody's RMS internship on the Event Response team describes monitoring tropical cyclones, floods, and wildfires and contributing to real-time modeling and reports as the event evolves. Between events, the same people improve models, clean exposure data, and prepare analyses for renewals.

How this differs from operational forecasting

The atmospheric science is familiar. The product and the audience are not. Operational forecasting is built around a specific place and time: what conditions are likely over the next hours or days, and whether a warning is warranted. Catastrophe risk work is built around a portfolio. The question is how a large set of possible events, including ones that have not happened yet, would affect a book of insured properties, and what that implies for pricing, capital, and reinsurance.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics notes that some forecast jobs involve shift work to provide 24-hour coverage, with extended hours during severe weather. Catastrophe risk roles are usually office-based analysis jobs, with deadlines around renewals, model updates, and live events, not a forecast shift schedule. A risk briefing also has to explain uncertainty in a loss number to underwriters, actuaries, and managers who may have little atmospheric science background.

Skills employers ask for

A bachelor's degree in meteorology or a related field is the usual starting credential. The BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook says atmospheric scientists typically need that degree, and that coursework often includes physics, mathematics, and computer programming.

Employers in this sector are more specific about the tools. The AMS insurance guide recommends programming in SQL, R, Python, or Matlab; a statistics course that covers probability, distributions, and return periods; and GIS training with ArcGIS or QGIS. It also recommends coursework in natural hazards and, if available, an introduction to insurance and reinsurance. Current postings match that list. The Berkshire Hathaway Specialty Insurance analyst role asks for a bachelor's or master's in meteorology or a related science, proficiency in Python, R, or MATLAB, and working knowledge of probability and statistics, with preferred experience in numerical weather prediction output, reanalysis, and observations. The Moody's internship asks for a working knowledge of Python, R, SQL, or Fortran, and lists ArcGIS or QGIS as preferred. A BHSI atmospheric science internship likewise asks for Python or R and statistics, and lists GIS experience as a plus. Climate data literacy matters because the models and the validation work depend on knowing what a reanalysis, a station record, or a radar dataset can and cannot represent.

Getting in

Internships and entry-level analyst roles are the usual door. The BLS notes that internship experience during college can be helpful. Summer internships at modeling firms, insurers, and brokers are advertised as project work on model development, event response, or risk evaluation. Some, like the BHSI atmospheric science internship, are aimed at graduate students. Others, including the Moody's internship, are open to students pursuing a meteorology or related undergraduate degree.

Analyst titles are worth a close look even when they do not say "meteorologist." An AMS career profile of a catastrophe modeling manager describes a first job as a data management analyst at an insurer that became a catastrophe modeling analyst role, with the insurance knowledge learned at work.

The science degree already covers the atmosphere. The applications still ask for programming used in analysis and for some GIS or other spatial work. Insurance vocabulary is usually learned on the job, but it helps to arrive already knowing what a portfolio, a return period, and a loss estimate mean.
