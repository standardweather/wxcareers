## Start with the 1340 series

NWS forecasters are hired under the Meteorology series, GS-1340. Before anyone reads your experience, HR checks your transcript against the [OPM qualification standard for the 1340 series](https://www.opm.gov/policy-data-oversight/classification-qualifications/general-schedule-qualification-standards/1300/meteorology-series-1340/). It is specific, and it is the first place applications fail.

You need a degree in meteorology, atmospheric science, or another natural science that includes at least 24 semester hours of meteorology or atmospheric science. Within those hours, the standard calls for:

- Six semester hours of atmospheric dynamics and thermodynamics
- Six semester hours of analysis and prediction of weather systems (synoptic and mesoscale)
- Three semester hours of physical meteorology
- Two semester hours of remote sensing of the atmosphere or instrumentation

On top of that, you need six semester hours of physics with at least one lab, three semester hours of ordinary differential equations, and nine semester hours of physical science coursework drawn from at least three areas such as statistics, chemistry, hydrology, physical oceanography, climatology, radiative transfer, or computer science. Dynamics, physics, and differential equations carry a calculus prerequisite. There is also a path that combines this coursework with experience or additional education.

My advice: open your transcript next to that list and map every course to a line. If your synoptic class was titled something odd, you want to know that now, not after a rejection. Upload the transcript the announcement asks for, every time.

## Entry grades and how pay is set

Most new forecasters come in around GS-5, GS-7, or GS-9. A bachelor's degree that meets the 1340 requirements generally qualifies you at GS-5. Under OPM's [General Schedule qualification standards](https://www.opm.gov/policy-data-oversight/classification-qualifications/general-schedule-qualification-standards/), GS-7 typically takes a year of graduate education or superior academic achievement as an undergraduate, and GS-9 typically takes a master's degree or two years of graduate study. Specialized experience at the next lower grade can also qualify you. Many announcements list several grades at once, so apply to every grade you meet.

Federal pay is public. The [2026 base General Schedule table](https://www.opm.gov/policy-data-oversight/pay-leave/salaries-wages/salary-tables/26Tables/html/GS.aspx) lists GS-5 step 1 at $34,799, GS-7 step 1 at $43,106, and GS-9 step 1 at $52,727. Those are base rates. Nearly every location adds locality pay on top, and the amount depends on where the office sits. Look up the actual area on OPM's [2026 locality pay tables](https://www.opm.gov/policy-data-oversight/pay-leave/salaries-wages/2026/general-schedule/) before you compare an offer to anything else.

## Finding and filtering announcements

The NWS posts its vacancies on USAJOBS, and its [careers page](https://www.weather.gov/careers/) links straight to current openings. On USAJOBS, search by the 1340 series rather than a job title, then filter by agency and location. Set up a saved search with email alerts. Some announcements stay open only a short time, and the alert is what keeps you from missing one.

Read the full announcement before you start. The "Qualifications" section spells out the specialized experience for each grade, and "How to Apply" tells you exactly which documents count. Treat it as the assignment rubric.

If you are still in school, look at the Pathways Program. The NWS describes it on the same careers page as paid work for students while they finish their degree, and it is one of the better ways to get federal experience on your resume early.

## The federal resume, now two pages

The old multi-page federal resume is gone. Under OPM's [guidance on the two-page resume limit](https://www.opm.gov/policy-data-oversight/hiring-information/merit-hiring-plan-resources/agency-guidance-on-the-two-page-limit-on-resume-length), agencies review only resumes of two pages or less, and USAJOBS has restricted resumes to two pages since September 27, 2025. Longer ones are removed from consideration.

Two pages still has to carry federal details. The USAJOBS Help Center's page on [how to write a resume for a federal job](https://usajobshelp.opm.gov/hc/en-us/articles/52839989720851-How-do-I-write-a-resume-for-a-federal-job) says each position should include the employer, job title, start and end dates with month and year, the hours you worked per week, and series and grade for any federal job. Do not skip hours per week. HR uses them to credit how much experience you actually have, and part-time work is prorated.

Then match the announcement. If it asks for experience issuing forecasts, interpreting radar and satellite data, or communicating hazards to partners, say so in those words, with a concrete example. A line like "Issued daily zone forecasts for a 12-county area during a student internship, 20 hours per week" does more than a paragraph about passion for weather. Cut anything unrelated to make room.

## Questionnaire self-ratings

Most applications include an occupational questionnaire, and the [USAJOBS application process page](https://usajobshelp.opm.gov/hc/en-us/articles/52846644688019-How-does-the-application-process-work) notes you can preview it under Required Documents. Your answers help sort you into categories such as minimally qualified and highest qualified.

Rate yourself honestly but fully. Many applicants undersell work they have done because they did it as students. If you have done the task, claim it, and make sure your resume shows where. A high rating with nothing in the resume to back it up is the kind of mismatch HR is looking for.

## Referral lists and what follows

After the announcement closes, HR reviews applications and sends the highest qualified to the hiring official. That is the referral. Being referred is a real milestone, but it does not guarantee an interview. Interviews may be by phone, video, panel, or in person, and there may be more than one round. A selection leads to a tentative offer, then a background investigation, then a final offer with a start date.

## Location and timeline

The NWS has offices across the country and its territories, and the [careers page](https://www.weather.gov/careers/) notes that most of its atmospheric scientists work in forecast offices, center weather service units, national centers, headquarters, and labs. Your odds improve with every location you are willing to accept. A first assignment somewhere you never planned to live is a common start, and a well-run small office is a fine place to learn the job.

Expect it to take time. Reviews start after the closing date, interviews depend on how many people are referred, and the background check comes after the offer. Keep applying while you wait, check your application status in your USAJOBS profile, and keep your contact information current.

The process is strict, but it is fair to people who follow it. Know your coursework, write to the announcement, and stay flexible on where you start.

When you are ready to look, the current federal and NWS openings are on the [WxCareers job listings](https://wxcareers.com/).
